## PART 1 — INSURANCE ADEQUACY: the "are you actually covered?" framework

Design rule for the app: the adequacy check must be willing to say "you're fine."
Each section below ends with the explicit test for when NO action is needed —
upsell-only logic is what users (rightly) distrust.

### 1.1 Life insurance

**Two sizing methods the industry actually uses:**

- **10–12x income rule (quick):** coverage = 10–12x gross annual income. An $80k
  earner → $800k–$960k. Fast sanity check, but blunt: it ignores debts,
  mortgage, kids, and spouse income. Treat as a floor, not a target.
- **DIME method (tailored):** Debt (non-mortgage: credit cards, auto, student
  loans) + Income (annual income × years of support needed, standard = 10 yrs;
  single-earner family with young kids = 12–15x; dual-earner no kids = 5–7x;
  single no kids = 0–3x) + Mortgage (remaining balance) + Education (~$100k per
  child) − existing coverage/savings. This is what agents actually recommend;
  10x is the reasonableness check.

Worked example: $25k debt + $75k × 15 yrs ($1.125M) + $280k mortgage + $100k
education = $1.53M, then subtract existing savings/coverage.

**Why term beats whole life for almost everyone:** needs peak during working
years and decline as savings grow and kids leave — term matches that shape.
Whole life costs roughly **10–15x term** for the same face amount because it
bundles lifelong coverage + cash value. Permanent insurance is defensible only
for: estate liquidity, business buy-sell funding, lifelong dependent care, or
specific tax-advantaged accumulation strategies.

**Actual cost (verified, late-2025/2026 rate surveys):** a healthy nonsmoking
30-year-old buying $500,000 of 20-year term pays roughly **$23/mo (woman) /
$30/mo (man)** — Policygenius Price Index, late 2025. Guardian 2025 data:
$23.50/mo woman, $28/mo man. MoneyGeek 2026: ~$20–30/mo. The 2025 Insurance
Barometer Study found adults ≤30 overestimate the cost of a $250k 20-year term
by **10–12x** — the single biggest barrier to coverage is misperception, not
price. Smokers pay 2–3x. Rates lock for the full term; waiting until 40 adds
~$4,320 over the 20-year term vs. buying at 30.

**The gap is real:** LIMRA 2024 — 42% of U.S. adults (~102 million) have no life
insurance or not enough; still above 100 million in 2025.

**The "you're fine" test:** single, no dependents, no debts anyone else depends
on, enough savings for final expenses → you do not need income-replacement life
insurance. Also fine: employer group coverage already ≥ your DIME number AND
the job is stable (group coverage dies with the job — that's the catch).

### 1.2 Disability insurance (statistically more important than life for young workers)

**The core stat (authoritative — SSA Office of the Chief Actuary, Actuarial
Note 2025.6):** for an insured worker attaining age 20 in 2025, the probability
of becoming disabled before normal retirement age (67) is **24%**, vs. **13%**
probability of dying. Disability is roughly **2x as likely as death** for a
young worker. CBPP: ~1 in 3 young workers will die or qualify for SSDI before
full retirement age. Yet disability insurance is treated as optional while life
insurance sits in nearly every plan — the comparison is backward.

**Why employer LTD alone is usually a gap:**
- Typical employer plan caps at **60% of base salary** — and excludes bonuses,
  commissions, and self-employment income from the definition.
- If the employer pays the premium, benefits are **taxable income**; a 60% gross
  benefit can be ~40% net. Individually owned policies (paid with after-tax
  dollars) pay **tax-free**.
- Not portable: coverage ends when you leave the job.
- Many group plans shift from "own occupation" to "any occupation" after
  ~24 months — benefits stop if you can do *any* job, not your own.
- Average LTD claim lasts **~3 years**; SSDI approves only ~30% of initial
  claims and pays ~$1,580/mo average (2025) — not a plan.

**What private DI costs:** **1–3% of income** is the standard range. A healthy
30-year-old pays roughly **$60–$150/mo** for solid individual coverage.
Riders (COLA, future purchase option, residual/partial) add 15–40% to the base
premium. The two riders that matter most for young buyers: **future purchase
option** (raise coverage without new underwriting as income grows) and **COLA**
(a benefit fixed at claim start erodes for a decade-long claim).

**The "you're fine" test:** you have own-occupation individual coverage sized
to your real income (including variable comp), with a future-purchase rider,
OR: no one depends on your income AND 6+ months of expenses saved AND your
employer plan covers ≥60% with a 90-day elimination period you can self-fund.
Self-employed with no coverage and thin savings → not fine; this is the
highest-risk profile.

- https://cbpp.org/research/social-security/social-security-disability-insurance-0

### 1.3 Renters insurance

**Cost (verified):** NerdWallet rate analysis (Jan 2026): **$151/yr (~$13/mo)**
for $30k personal property + $100k liability + $500 deductible. Broader
industry range: **$15–25/mo** ($180–300/yr). One covered claim ($3k–$5k typical;
$13k–$15k for fires/major water) repays **10–50+ years** of premiums.

**What people don't know it covers:**
- **Personal liability** ($100k standard, up to $500k available): if a guest is
  hurt in your unit or you damage someone else's property — this is the real
  value, not the stuff.
- **Loss of use / additional living expenses:** hotel, food, laundry, storage
  while displaced by a covered peril.
- **Medical payments to others** (guest medical, ~$1k) regardless of fault.
- What it does NOT cover: the building itself (landlord's policy), flooding,
  earthquakes.

**The gap:** only **about half of U.S. renters** carry it. The common myth:
"the landlord's insurance covers my stuff" — it covers the structure only.

**The "you're fine" test:** you already carry ≥$100k liability + loss-of-use
with a deductible you can afford → fine. No renter with <$1k in replaceable
property AND no guests AND a landlord requiring nothing is meaningfully
exposed — but at $13–15/mo, the liability coverage alone justifies it for
almost everyone. Skip only if you genuinely have no liability exposure and
nothing worth replacing.

### 1.4 Auto insurance

**State minimums vs. recommended:** typical state minimum is **25/50/25**
($25k bodily injury per person / $50k per accident / $25k property damage).
Expert consensus recommendation: **100/300/100** minimum for anyone with assets
or regular driving; **250/500/100** for homeowners/families; umbrella beyond
that. A serious crash routinely exceeds $300k — everything above your limit is
personally owed. Higher limits cost **only slightly more per month** than
minimums.

**Uninsured/underinsured motorist (UM/UIM) — the gap people miss:** UM pays
*your* medical bills and repairs when the at-fault driver has no (or too
little) insurance. IRC data (via Insurify, 2026): **28.2% of Mississippi
drivers are uninsured** — the worst state. MoneyGeek's 2026 compilation uses
IRC's *Uninsured and Underinsured Motorists: 2017–2023* dataset for state
rates. UM coverage usually costs a few dollars/month and should mirror your
liability limits — note some carriers require matching liability limits to
sell higher UMBI.

**The "you're fine" test:** 100/300/100 + UM/UIM matching + collision/comprehensive
if the car is worth >~$5k or financed → fine. State minimums are defensible only
if you have genuinely no assets to protect, drive rarely, and carry UM — even
then, the price delta to 100/300/100 is small enough that "fine" is a stretch.

### 1.5 Health insurance: HDHP vs. PPO decision framework

**2026 IRS numbers (verified):** HDHP minimum deductible **$1,700 individual /
$3,400 family**; HDHP out-of-pocket maximum cap **$8,500 / $17,000**. HSA
contribution limits **$4,300 individual / $8,550 family** (+$1,000 catch-up at
55+). HSA triple tax advantage: deductible in, tax-free growth, tax-free out
for medical — unmatched by any other account; after 65, withdraws penalty-free
for any purpose (taxed as income, like a Traditional IRA).

**Decision framework (total annual cost = premiums + expected out-of-pocket −
employer HSA seed − tax savings on HSA contributions):**
- **HDHP wins when:** healthy, few doctor visits, can fund the HSA, employer
  seeds the HSA. Premium savings (~$1,000+/yr vs. PPO) + employer HSA
  contribution + tax deduction on contributions often beat the PPO even in a
  bad year — one Bogleheads 2026 analysis had the HDHP ~$7,800 ahead before
  any medical spending. Max OOP is the hard ceiling on the downside.
- **PPO wins when:** chronic conditions, regular specialists/prescriptions,
  planned surgery or pregnancy, or you can't cash-flow the deductible. The
  PPO's predictability (copays, lower deductible) beats the HDHP when expected
  spending is high and steady.

**The "you're fine" test:** EITHER plan is "fine" if you've run the
total-cost math above for *your* expected usage and the max-OOP exposure fits
your emergency fund. The failure mode isn't picking the wrong plan — it's
never doing the comparison and defaulting to last year's choice. Red flag:
HDHP without funding the HSA = paying the high deductible with none of the
tax benefit.
